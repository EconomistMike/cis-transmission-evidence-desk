Overview 
The Australian Energy Regulator (AER) is responsible for the economic regulation of 
electricity distribution and transmission systems in all states and territories except Western 
Australia. We exist to ensure energy consumers are better off, now and in the future. 
Consumers are at the heart of our work, and we focus on ensuring a secure, reliable, and 
affordable energy future for Australia as we transition to net zero emissions.  
In 2021, the NSW Government appointed us to be the economic regulator of infrastructure 
projects under the Electricity Infrastructure Investment Act 2020 (NSW) (EII Act). The EII Act 
enables the NSW Electricity Infrastructure Roadmap (the Roadmap) which sets out a plan for 
the development of Renewable Energy Zones (REZ) in NSW that will allow multiple 
renewable generation and storage projects to be grouped into specific locations to achieve a 
number of NSW Government policy objectives. 
Hunter Central Coast REZ network infrastructure project 
The Hunter-Central Coast (HCC) REZ was declared by the NSW Minister for Energy under 
section 19(1) of the EII Act in December 2022. The HCC REZ network infrastructure project 
(HCC Project) will provide an additional one gigawatt of new network transfer capacity by 
2028 to support renewable energy generation and storage within the HCC REZ region. 
Ausgrid was selected by the Infrastructure Planner, Energy Corporation of NSW (EnergyCo), 
as the preferred Network Operator for the HCC Project in December 2024. Subsequently, in 
April 2025, AusEnergy Services Ltd (the Consumer Trustee – formerly known as AEMO 
Services Limited), authorised Ausgrid to carry out the HCC project.1  
On 16 May 2025, we received a non-contestable revenue proposal from Ausgrid for the 
HCC Project for the regulatory control period 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2031 (2026–31 period). 
Ausgrid proposed total capital expenditure (capex) of $590.8 million ($2025–26) and revenue 
of $200.3 million ($ nominal). In early July 2025, Ausgrid submitted an updated revenue 
proposal with a revised total capex of $604.2 million ($2025–26) and revenue of $203.6 
million ($ nominal) to correct an error in its proposal. We accepted Ausgrid’s updated 
proposal as a one-off situation, on the basis Ausgrid had made a genuine error by omitting 
capex related overheads but had identified the issue at an early stage of the revenue 
determination process. This meant that stakeholders still had an ability to comment on the 
updated revenue proposal.  
The HCC Project is the second non-contestable determination we have made. It follows our 
2024–29 final decision for the Waratah Super Battery non-contestable project (WSB Project) 
made in December 2023.2

Our role in making revenue determinations for NSW REZ network 
infrastructure projects  
Our role as a regulator under the EII Act is to apply a transmission efficiency test (TET) and 
determine the amounts payable to Network Operators3 authorised by the Consumer Trustee 
or authorised (or directed) by the NSW Minister for Energy to carry out network projects. We 
do not decide the size or timing of network infrastructure projects, as these responsibilities 
sits with the Consumer Trustee and the Infrastructure Planner. 
A revenue determination made for a non-contestable process involves an assessment of the 
Network Operator’s proposed costs and revenue to ensure only the prudent, efficient and 
reasonable costs of delivering the project are recovered from NSW electricity consumers. We 
do this assessment in accordance with the requirements set out in the EII Act, the Electricity 
Infrastructure Investment Regulation 2021 (NSW) (EII Regulation) and the process set out in 
our Transmission Efficiency Test and revenue determination guideline for non-contestable 
network infrastructure projects (Non-contestable Guideline).  
Unlike revenue determinations made under the National Electricity Rules (NER), we only 
have 126 business days to make a non-contestable revenue determination under the EII 
Regulation.4 In making our revenue determination for the HCC Project we have assessed 
Ausgrid’s updated revenue proposal in accordance with these requirements.   
Our final decision on Ausgrid’s updated revenue proposal 
Our final decision is that Ausgrid can recover $189.6 million ($ nominal) for the 2026–31 
period. This is $14.0 million (6.9%) less than Ausgrid’s updated revenue proposal. Our final 
decision accepts most elements of Ausgrid’s proposal, namely: 
• We have included an alternative capex estimate of $615.1 million5 ($2025–26) 
($284.5 million in pre-period capex and $330.6 million in forecast capex), which is 
$10.9 million higher than Ausgrid’s proposal. The increase in capex reflects our decisions 
to amend the ‘land acquisition and planning’ adjustment mechanism, resulting in an 
increase to capex, and accept Ausgrid’s updated forecast of ‘enabling activities’, for 
costs associated with connecting the HCC Project to the broader transmission network. 
• We accept Ausgrid’s proposed total operating expenditure (opex) of $16.9 million 
($2025–26) as we found it to be prudent, efficient and reasonable. Our bottom-up 
assessment found that these activities are necessary, costs are reasonably estimated, 
and shared costs are estimated in line with Ausgrid’s cost allocation methodology. 
The decrease in total revenue in this final decision compared to Ausgrid’s updated revenue 
proposal is mainly driven by:  
• Updates to financial inputs for inflation and the rate of return which has (keeping all other 
inputs constant) decreased revenue by $6.2 million

1 Background 
1.1 Project context 
On 9 December 2022, the NSW Minister for Energy declared the HCC REZ.10 The HCC 
geographical area includes Upper Hunter, Muswellbrook, Singleton, Dungog, Cessnock, 
Maitland, Port Stephens, Newcastle, Lake Macquarie and the Central Coast.11   
This HCC REZ declaration was amended on 2 December 2024 to extend the specified 
network infrastructure covered by the declaration to include network infrastructure outside 
the specified geographic area and connecting Somersby Zone Station and Berowra Zone 
Station.12 
The HCC Project involves the construction and operation of network infrastructure to enable 
the connection of new renewable energy generation and storage projects in the HCC REZ. It 
will provide an additional 1 GW of network transfer capacity and will consist of three portions:  
• Portion 1: Upper Hunter secondary systems modernisation (providing 350MW additional 
network capacity), scheduled for completion in late 2025.  
• Portion 2: Muswellbrook network rearrangement, Singleton to Kurri 132kV link, and 
installation of a communications link across the Hawkesbury River (providing a cumulative 
total of 630 MW additional network capacity), scheduled for completion in June 2028.  
• Portion 3: Construction of a new Antiene 132kV switching station and Antiene to 
Singleton 132kV link (providing a cumulative total of 1 GW of additional network 
capacity), scheduled for completion in July 2028.  
1.2 Authorisation of the Project 
EnergyCo is required to assess and make recommendations about REZ network 
infrastructure projects to the Consumer Trustee. In October 2024, EnergyCo recommended 
Ausgrid as the preferred Network Operator to the Consumer Trustee for the HCC Project. In 
December 2024, Ausgrid signed a Commitment Deed13 with EnergyCo to deliver the HCC 
Project by mid-2028.  
In December 2024, EnergyCo submitted an Infrastructure Planner Recommendation Report 
to the Consumer Trustee that recommended the Consumer Trustee authorise Ausgrid to 
carry out the HCC Project. On 17 April 2025, the Consumer Trustee, AusEnergy Services 
Limited authorised Ausgrid to develop, construct, own, control and operate the HCC Project

2 Revenue determination process 
We are a regulator under the EII Act. One of our functions is to determine the amount 
payable to a Network Operator for carrying out a network infrastructure project.18 There are 
two ways a Network Operator may be selected to undertake a network infrastructure project. 
1. Under a contestable process, a Network Operator is selected following a competitive 
assessment procurement process conducted by the Infrastructure Planner.  
2. Under a non-contestable process, a Network Operator may be selected directly by the 
Infrastructure Planner. 
Our revenue determination for the HCC Project is made under the non-contestable process. 
A revenue determination made for a non-contestable process involves us assessing the 
Network Operator’s forecast costs to ensure the prudent, efficient, and reasonable costs of 
delivering the project are recovered.19 Prior to making a revenue determination, we must also 
ensure under section 38(6) of the EII Act that the prudent, efficient and reasonable capital 
costs for development and construction of a REZ network infrastructure project does not 
exceed the maximum amount, if any, notified to us by the Consumer Trustee under section 
31(2).20 This maximum amount is confidential.21  
In making our first revenue determination for the HCC Project we have followed the process 
set out in our Non-contestable Guideline and included in our decision those matters set out in 
the EII Act, the EII Regulation and our Non-contestable Guideline.22 The components of our 
decision in section 14 of this final decision sets out how we have addressed the requirements 
of the EII Act, EII Regulation and the EII Chapter 6A in making our final decision.  
Our Non-contestable Guideline sets out the process we are required to follow when making a 
non-contestable revenue determination under the EII Act.23 This process substantially 
reflects the process that applies to transmission network service providers  under Chapter 6A 
of the NER.24  
In accordance with sections 38(4) and (6) of the EII Act, the amount we have calculated as 
the prudent, efficient and reasonable capital costs for the development and construction of 
the HCC Project is lower than the confidential maximum amount (maximum capital cost) 
notified to the AER by the Consumer Trustee.

2.2 Preliminary position paper  
In our 2024 review of the Non-contestable Guideline, we removed the requirement for us to 
make a draft decision as well as the requirement for the Network Operator to submit a 
revised proposal. Instead, we replaced the draft decision with a preliminary position paper 
that is to be published approximately 55 business days after receiving a compliant revenue 
proposal. The preliminary position paper is designed to focus on identifying and discussing 
key issues arising from a Network Operator’s revenue proposal.  
On 7 August 2025, we published a preliminary position paper for the HCC Project27 and 
sought submissions by 10 September 2025.28 In our preliminary position paper, we focused 
on Ausgrid’s proposed adjustment mechanisms related to ‘procurement induced cost 
uncertainty events’ as we considered this component represented a significant point of 
difference between Ausgrid’s revenue proposal and our preliminary position.29 However, our 
ability to discuss our position for these adjustment mechanisms was restricted at the time 
due to Ausgrid’s non-disclosure claims on the names and definition of most of these events. 
In summary, our other preliminary positions were that we were likely to:  
• change the depreciation treatment of IPFs associated with Ausgrid’s early works capex 
to align with the economic life of these assets rather than the Project term. 
• accept Ausgrid’s proposal to modify the CESS to include pre-period expenditure but not 
accept Ausgrid’s proposed modification to the CESS to exclude social licence 
expenditure.  
• accept Ausgrid’s proposed capex if it is found prudent, efficient, and reasonable, 
including further review of its proposed social licence expenditure. We also indicated that 
we would update capex for ‘enabling activities’, where new information indicated this cost 
was likely to increase.  
• make a decision on applying efficiency benefit sharing scheme (EBSS) at the end of the 
2026–31 period.  
• accept Ausgrid’s approach to calculating total revenue, schedule of payments, regulatory 
asset base, rate of return, regulatory depreciation, and opex.

3 Our Final Decision 
3.1 Key components of our final decision 
The foundation of our regulatory approach is a benchmark incentive framework to setting 
maximum revenues: once regulated revenues are set for the 5-year period, a network that 
keeps its actual costs below the regulatory forecast of costs retains part of the benefit. This 
provides an incentive for service providers to become more efficient over time.32 It delivers 
benefits to consumers as efficient costs are revealed over time and drives lower cost 
benchmarks in subsequent regulatory control periods. By only allowing efficient costs in our 
approved revenues, we ensure consumers pay no more than necessary for the safe and 
reliable delivery of electricity. 
Our assessment of total revenues under the EII Act, is based on a ‘building block’ approach 
which looks at the following five cost components (see Figure 3.1): 
1. return on the Regulatory Asset Base (RAB) – or return on capital, to compensate 
investors for the opportunity cost of funds invested in this business. 
2. depreciation of the RAB – or return of capital, to return the initial investment to investors 
over time. 
3. forecast opex – the operating, maintenance, and other non-capital expenses, incurred in 
the provision of network services. 
4. revenue increments/decrements – resulting from the application of incentive schemes in 
previous periods. As this is the first determination for the HCC Project, there are no 
revenue increments nor decrements.  
5. estimated cost of corporate income tax.

7 Rate of Return 
The return each business is to receive on its RAB, known as the ‘return on capital’, is a key 
driver of proposed revenues. We calculate the regulated return on capital by applying a rate 
of return to the value of the RAB.67   
We estimate the rate of return by combining the returns of the two sources of funds for 
investment: equity and debt. The allowed rate of return provides the business with a return 
on capital to service the interest on its loans and give a return on equity to investors. 
We also make an estimate of expected inflation over the next 5 years. Alongside our nominal 
estimate of the rate of return, these determine the effective real return that will be provided to 
investors over time. 
Our assessment approach for the rate of return is detailed in section 4 of our assessment 
approach guidance note.68

7.2.2 Transition to the trailing average portfolio return on debt 
The 2022 Instrument provides for a gradual transition into the 10-year trailing average 
portfolio return on debt.83 This transition period commences in the first regulatory year for 
which the return on debt is calculated using a trailing average for the first time for the 
relevant regulated service. The transition takes 10 years to complete. 
In the first year, there is only one return on debt estimate, so it is given 100% weight. In each 
subsequent year, a new estimate is added to the portfolio and given 10% weight, while the 
weight on the earliest year reduces by 10%. Eventually, after 10 years, a rolling 10-year 
window is established. 
For Ausgrid across the 2026–31 regulatory control period, this means the weights are as 
follows:  
• 2026–27: 100% on 2026–27 
• 2027–28: 90% on 2026–27, 10% on 2027–28 
• 2028–29: 80% on 2026–27, 10% on 2027–28, 10% on 2028–29 
• 2029–30: 70% on 2026–27, 10% on 2027–28, 10% on 2028–29, 10% on 2029–30 
• 2030–31: 60% on 2026–27, 10% on 2027–28, 10% on 2028–29, 10% on 2029–30, 10% 
on 2030–31 
This pattern continues until the full 10-year rolling window is established in 2035–36. 
7.2.3 Pre-period rate of return 
The pre-period WACC is used to capitalise Ausgrid’s pre-period expenditure to form an 
opening RAB at the start of its regulatory control period.  
Under the EII framework, a Network Operator may be required to incur expenditure prior to 
the first regulatory determination. For a non-contestable process, a Network Operator can 
only recover its investments through our revenue determinations. As such, we consider it 
reasonable for a Network Operator to capitalise its efficient, prudent and reasonable pre-
period expenditure to form an opening RAB as at the start of the relevant regulatory control 
period.84 We typically apply a nominal WACC when capitalising these costs to compensate 
the Network Operator for the delay in revenue recovery. However, while we are bound by the 
2022 Instrument in determining an overall rate of return when making a determination, it 
does not address the specific situation where there is expenditure in advance of the first 
regulatory control period.  
We expect financing costs during the pre-period years to be reflective of the broader 
business’ rate of return, reflecting the opportunity cost of obtaining financing in these years.

9 Capital Expenditure 
Capital expenditure (capex) refers to investment made for the development and construction 
of network infrastructure. This investment mostly relates to assets with long asset lives 
(30-50 years), the costs of which are recovered over several regulatory control periods. 
Under the regulatory framework, Ausgrid’s revenue proposal must include the total forecast 
capex that it considers is required to comply with all regulatory requirements associated with 
the provision of regulated network services and maintain the safety of the network 
infrastructure project through the supply of regulated network services (the capex 
objectives).109 We must determine whether or not we are satisfied that Ausgrid’s forecast 
capex (including pre period capex) reasonably reflects the capex criteria.110 In doing so, we 
must have regard to the capex factors specified in EII Chapter 6A.111 If we are not satisfied, 
we must set out the reasons for this decision and an estimate of the total capex that we are 
satisfied reasonably reflects the capex criteria, taking into account the capex factors.112  
Our assessment approach for capex is detailed in section 6 of our assessment approach 
guidance note.113

payments required to be made to EnergyCo by the Network Operator under a contractual

10 Operating Expenditure 
Operating expenditure (opex) refers to operating, maintenance and other non-capital 
expenses. Forecast opex for regulated network services is one of the building blocks we use 
to determine a network operator’s total revenue cap.138  
Under the regulatory framework, Ausgrid’s revenue proposal must include the total forecast 
opex that it considers is required to meet or manage the expected demand for regulated 
network services, comply with all applicable regulatory requirements and maintain the safety

we must have regard to the opex factors specified in EII Chapter 6A.141 If we are not 
satisfied, we must set out the reasons for this decision and an estimate of the total opex that 
we are satisfied reasonably reflects the opex criteria, taking into account the opex factors.142

13 Adjustment mechanisms 
Adjustment mechanisms are pre-defined circumstances that allow a Network Operator to 
request the adjustment of any amount set out in a revenue determination.182 These 
mechanisms are intended to account for risks that are outside of a Network Operator’s 
control, and which cannot be efficiently mitigated through other means. 
Our approach to considering revenue adjustment mechanisms is described in our revenue 
determination guideline for non-contestable projects183 and our Guidance note on the AER’s 
EII Assessment Approach for non-contestable revenue determinations.184  
In our preliminary position paper and supplementary appendix, we highlighted Ausgrid’s 
proposed procurement-induced cost uncertainty events as a focus issue for our assessment 
and encouraged consumers to provide submissions. Our final decision is to include all 25 of 
Ausgrid’s proposed adjustment mechanisms in our revenue determination but amend 3 of its 
7 procurement-induced cost uncertainty events to align with our incentive-based regulatory 
framework. 
Our role in reviewing and amending adjustment mechanisms under the NSW non-
contestable EII framework 
Our role as the Regulator in a non-contestable EII process is to determine the amount 
payable to the Network Operator for carrying out the network infrastructure project based on 
an assessment of its revenue proposal and supporting information.185 A Network Operator 
may include in its revenue proposal mechanisms to adjust any amount provided for in our 
revenue determination. The EII Regulation provides the AER with discretion as to whether or 
not to include an adjustment mechanism in its determination or potentially include it in an 
amended form.  
The adjustment mechanisms submitted by Ausgrid in its revenue proposal reflected its prior 
negotiations with EnergyCo which underpinned the signing of the Commitment Deed in 
December 2024. We note that, as a general principle, negotiations between a Network 
Operator (in this case, Ausgrid) and the Infrastructure Planner (EnergyCo) do not constrain 
the AER when making its determination under a non-contestable framework. We act as an 
independent assessor186 of whether the proposed adjustment mechanisms will contribute to 
the recovery of prudent, efficient and reasonable costs.187 We also have regard to the 
incentive properties of the proposed adjustment mechanisms, including whether they 
promote economically efficient behaviour by the Network Operator.188

13.2.5 Ausgrid’s submission to our preliminary position paper 
In its submission to our preliminary position paper, Ausgrid noted that our use of a delayed 
capex forecast adjustment mechanism approach supports regulatory certainty, customer 
affordability and preserves efficiency incentives.196 Ausgrid’s submission also suggested how 
a proposed delayed capex forecast process might work in practice.197 We consider that 
Ausgrid’s suggested delayed capex forecast adjustment approach balances its need to 
recover its prudent, efficient and reasonable costs, while reducing consumer exposure to 
unbounded risks. Ausgrid’s submission reflects constructive discussions with AER staff and 
aligns with our expectations. 
In our final decision, we have adopted much of Ausgrid’s suggested process, subject to a few 
key differences. These differences are: 
• Ausgrid will be required to write a public letter to the AER if the trigger milestone has not 
occurred by its estimated date explaining the reason for the delay; and 
• if the trigger milestone occurs after the estimated date, it is at the AER’s discretion to 
decide to reject an application for adjustment made by Ausgrid under that adjustment 
event, having regard to specific criteria (outlined in section 13.2.4); and 
• We have not accepted Ausgrid’s suggested CESS modification in relation to the delayed 
capex forecast. Ausgrid’s proposed modification and our reasoning for our decision is set 
out in section 12 of this document. 
In its submission to our preliminary position paper, Ausgrid proposed using a project 
milestone rather than a specific calendar date as the trigger for submitting its delayed capex 
forecast. It submitted that a milestone trigger is anchored in a tangible project outcome that 
directly shapes expenditure requirements.  
We consider that Ausgrid’s suggestion to use a trigger milestone rather than a specific date 
would allow for prudent and efficient costs to be estimated at the time when they are able to 
be reliably estimated. As mentioned in section 13.2.4, the trigger milestone occurs when 
Ausgrid provides a notice to the AER confirming that it has issued its final designs to 
contractors and has finalised engagement with those contractors regarding any impacts 
arising from final designs. This definition provides Ausgrid with control over the timing of 
when this trigger milestone occurs. We consider this is appropriate to allow Ausgrid sufficient 
time to negotiate its costs with its contractors.  
We consider it is best for consumers for the delayed capex forecast to be forecast earlier in 
the regulatory control period, as this applies incentive pressure to Ausgrid sooner. However, 
the timing of a milestone trigger could move and thus allow the delayed capex forecast to be

13.2.7 Stakeholder involvement in the delayed capex forecast 
adjustment  
Stakeholders play a vital role in shaping our revenue determinations. We consider that 
stakeholder engagement in terms of adjustment mechanisms is most valuable during our 
revenue determination process. This is because they are able to influence the scope and 
operation of the adjustment mechanism.  
In our assessment of an application for a revenue adjustment, including a delayed capex 
forecast adjustment, we are primarily focused on considering the prudency, efficiency and 
reasonableness of the costs we have already determined to be within the scope of the 
mechanism. While we acknowledge stakeholder submissions indicating an interest in being 
consulted on adjustment decisions, we have very limited time in which to complete our 
assessment, and allowing time for additional stakeholder consultation would further restrict 
the assessment time available to us. Given the limited time, the fixed scope of the 
mechanism, and the technical nature of the costs involved, we consider that effective 
stakeholder consultation during the application assessment process is generally not feasible. 
However, we reserve the right to conduct targeted consultation in assessing an adjustment 
proposal if we consider it necessary.

In our preliminary position paper, we considered the application of a cap as an option for

If we apply an individual or aggregate cap on adjustment mechanisms that is too low,

a result of carrying out a REZ infrastructure project (as the AER is required to take into 
account under s.37(1)(a) of the EII Act). Conversely, if we apply a cap that is too high, we 
risk not providing Ausgrid with sufficient incentive to incur only its efficient expenditure (as 
the AER is required to take into account under s.37(1)(b) of the EII Act). Such a cap would 
be of limited benefit to consumers. However, we consider that if a maximum expenditure cap 
is applied at the appropriate level, it can limit risk for consumers and provide incentive for the 
Network Operator to incur only prudent, efficient and reasonable costs.  
Given the extent of uncertainty around the HCC Project design, any cap would need to be 
very high. In this context, applying a cap would provide a limit on transfer of risk to 
consumers; but may represent more of a theoretical limit and provide little practical incentive 
for Ausgrid to constrain costs. In our final decision, we have not applied an expenditure cap 
to any of Ausgrid’s proposed adjustment mechanisms.
